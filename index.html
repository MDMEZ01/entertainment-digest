📈 Today's Deep Dive

Why Britain's TV Producers Are Waging a Quiet War Over Who Owns the Next "MasterChef"

Why It Matters
  • The clash over I.P. ownership between U.K. public broadcasters (the BBC and Channel 4, which by law grant producers rights to their programs) and U.S. streamers (Netflix and Amazon, which don't) determines whether producers can build billion-dollar catalog businesses or remain permanent for-hire vendors.
  • Banijay's Patrick Holland is betting on distribution scale via its All3Media merger, while Nutopia's Jane Root is bypassing broadcasters and streamers altogether — building star-driven shows with Will Smith, Trevor Noah and Jeff Goldblum directly for YouTube, a sign the cable-to-streaming disruption cycle is already spawning its next insurgent.
  • Raw's Dimitri Doganis says a documentary like "Tinder Swindler" has reportedly been watched by "half a billion people" — yet his studio doesn't own a piece of it, calling the dynamic "an endless source of both pride and pain."
The Money
  • Netflix pays as little as $1.2M-$1.5M for true-crime documentaries, per Doganis, versus vastly higher scripted budgets — yet "the return on investment is huge," illustrating why streamers under-price unscripted formats even while reaping outsized audiences.
  • Banijay's "MasterChef" format, now produced in 70 territories, shows what U.K. producers can build when they retain I.P. — a model the BBC's shrinking commissioning budget increasingly can't fund at the scale streamers can.
Historical Context
  • The dynamic echoes how U.S. studios bought out top TV showrunners' backend participation in the 2010s: producers again find "upfront money looks like cheap money" a decade later, per Doganis.
Watch For
  • Whether Root's YouTube-first, star-owned model becomes a viable "sell it after" pipeline that pressures Netflix and Amazon to offer U.K. producers equity stakes in what they make.
  • The BBC's charter renewal fight in 2027, and whether U.K. regulators impose "forced prominence" rules requiring streamers to feature public-service broadcasters more prominently.
