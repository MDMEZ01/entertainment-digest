📈 Today’s Deep Dive

Zaslav's Billion-Dollar Options: How the WBD Merger Delay Became a Financial Chess Match

Why It Matters
  • What started as a scheduling problem has hardened into a real financial option for David Zaslav — the delay is no longer just costly, it's a lever both sides are now consciously calculating around.
  • The fallout is already visible in how the two companies behave: Paramount and WBD are racing separate, competing ‘Nightmare on Elm Street’ remakes instead of coordinating, evidence they're still operating as rivals rather than a company-in-waiting.
  • Regulatory momentum is compounding in parallel — California's COMPETE Act could hand plaintiffs and regulators a durable new tool to challenge future media dealmaking well beyond this one case.
The Money
  • A quarterly “ticking fee” adds $0.25/share to WBD's price for every quarter the deal doesn't close, pushing the effective offer toward $31.75/share (roughly $113B) the longer Paramount Skydance waits.
  • If the deal isn't done by the drop-dead date, Zaslav can walk away and collect a $7 billion breakup fee from Paramount Skydance — though he'd forfeit whatever personal windfall closing the deal would have delivered.
Historical Context
  • The structure echoes AT&T's multi-year fight to close Time Warner, where a similar ticking fee became a bargaining chip that kept both sides negotiating rather than walking away outright.
Watch For
  • Whether Zaslav's team signals it's leaning toward the breakup-fee option as the deadline nears, or keeps banking the quarterly increases instead.
  • Whether California's COMPETE Act clears the state Senate before this case resolves, which would reshape the legal landscape for the next wave of media consolidation regardless of how this deal ends.
